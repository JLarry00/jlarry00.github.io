# IT professional marketing

Market a technical professional without turning them into a product, influencer, or list of technologies. The goal is to make the right audiences notice, understand, believe, investigate, and contact the person for relevant opportunities.

## Preserve the decision hierarchy

Classify inputs before acting:

1. **Objectives and constraints** are binding: career direction, target opportunities, privacy, language, accessibility, time, budget, and publication limits.
2. **Verified evidence** constrains what can be promised.
3. **Accepted strategy decisions** stay in force until deliberately reopened.
4. **Tastes and tactical opinions** are hypotheses to test against audience, evidence, differentiation, maintainability, and current research.

Do not flatter the user by accepting a weak idea. Do not override a legitimate professional objective because the evidence is still developing. Instead, distinguish the current claim from the direction and define what proof would close the gap.

When claims, project maturity, ownership, or publication safety matter, also use `.agents/skills/technical-portfolio-evidence/SKILL.md`.

## Follow the marketing workflow

### 1. Define the job of the professional presence

Identify:

- the opportunity sought now;
- the primary and secondary audiences;
- what each audience should understand and believe;
- the next useful action each audience should take;
- what the presence is not trying to achieve.

Do not assume that audience growth, posting cadence, followers, newsletters, speaking, or consulting are goals. Treat them as optional tactics.

### 2. Build an audience map

Separate at least the recruiter or sourcer, the hiring decision-maker, and the technical evaluator when they are relevant. Add peers, collaborators, clients, or communities only when they serve the objective.

For each audience capture:

- their decision and time horizon;
- the question they need answered;
- the evidence they can realistically inspect;
- likely objections or uncertainty;
- the appropriate channel and next action.

Read [audiences-and-funnel.md](references/audiences-and-funnel.md) for the decision journey and audience lenses.

### 3. Define a defensible market position

Create the position from:

- current professional identity;
- demonstrated domain or system focus;
- target direction;
- relevant problems the person can help solve;
- distinctive combination of experience, judgment, and perspective;
- proof that makes the position believable.

For Agentic AI or another aspirational focus, make the direction visible without promoting it to demonstrated expertise. Prefer a memorable combination of problem, system quality, and evidence over a broad title or a framework list.

Generate two or three materially different positioning options when the direction is not settled. Explain audience fit, advantages, risks, required proof, and what each option deprioritizes. Recommend one.

### 4. Build the message architecture

Define:

- one central impression to retain;
- a concise positioning line;
- three or four message pillars;
- proof attached to every pillar;
- a professional story that explains the direction without narrating the complete CV;
- likely objections and honest answers;
- primary and secondary calls to action.

Use concrete nouns and verbs. Avoid generic authority language, invented uniqueness, empty superlatives, and unverified market claims. Read [message-and-channels.md](references/message-and-channels.md) before drafting public copy or assigning channel roles.

### 5. Design the channel system

Give each channel a distinct function:

- website: synthesis, differentiation, selected evidence, and routes to depth;
- GitHub: inspectable technical proof and reproducibility;
- LinkedIn: discovery, context, vocabulary, and current professional signal;
- CV: opportunity-specific chronology and evidence;
- interview: interactive verification and judgment.

Keep the factual core consistent while adapting depth, order, and wording. Do not reproduce the same document everywhere.

### 6. Define conversion and measurement

Map the intended path:

`discover → understand → believe → investigate → contact`

Choose calls to action proportional to the objective, such as viewing a case study, inspecting a repository, downloading a CV, or starting a professional conversation. Use qualified signals rather than vanity metrics. Examples include relevant inbound conversations, technical reviews, interview progression, project inspection, or replies from the intended audience.

Never invent numerical benchmarks or claim that a tactic caused an outcome without evidence.

## Deliver a decision document

Return the parts relevant to the request:

1. objective and scope;
2. audience map;
3. confirmed facts, strategic proposals, pending decisions, and private inputs;
4. positioning options and recommendation;
5. message hierarchy and proof map;
6. channel roles and audience journey;
7. calls to action and meaningful signals;
8. risks, counterarguments, and evidence gaps;
9. optional experiments with a review date.

Make content strategy optional. When it is useful, derive a small number of themes from owned work and actual perspective. Do not prescribe a posting quota, personal disclosure, controversy, or an influencer persona.

## Release gate

Do not approve a strategy when:

- it could describe most people in the target field;
- the promised value exceeds the evidence;
- a framework or tool list substitutes for a professional position;
- every audience receives the same message;
- a tactic exists without a reason or target audience;
- the website becomes a long CV or a social-content machine;
- the call to action is unclear, disproportionate, or privacy-invasive;
- current market assumptions are undated or unsupported;
- the recommendation merely repeats the user's initial preference.
